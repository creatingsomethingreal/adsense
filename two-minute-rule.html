Make distraction inconvenient

A Quiet Hours essay · 4 min read

We tend to frame distraction as a failure of character. You picked up the phone again; you must lack discipline. This framing is not only cruel, it is wrong — and because it is wrong, it leads to solutions that never work. Willpower is a terrible defense against a device engineered by thousands of people to defeat exactly that willpower.

The better model is friction. You are far more sensitive to small inconveniences than you think. Most distraction is not a considered choice; it is a reflex that happens in the gap between an impulse and an action. Widen that gap by even a few seconds, and a surprising share of the reflex simply dissolves before it becomes behaviour.

Add steps, not rules

So stop trying to want it less, and start making it harder to reach. Log out of the account so that returning requires a password. Move the app off the home screen, then out of the first folder, then off the phone entirely. Leave the device in another room while you work. None of these require discipline in the moment — that is the whole point. They spend a little effort once, in a calm moment, so that you do not have to spend willpower repeatedly in weak ones.

The settings that do the quiet work

Two changes punch above their weight. Switching your phone to greyscale strips out the saturated colour that makes feeds compelling; a grey screen is simply less interesting to a brain wired for bright reward, and pickup frequency tends to fall without any conscious effort on your part. And turning off all but the most essential notifications converts your device from something that interrupts you into something you consult — a profound shift in who is in charge.

Discipline is what you reach for when your environment is working against you. Fix the environment and you need far less of it.

The goal is not a monastic life with no screens. It is an honest one, where the easy path and the good path point in the same direction. Arrange the friction once, deliberately, and your attention stops being something you have to defend hour by hour. It becomes, quietly, the default again.
